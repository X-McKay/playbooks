# 5.1 Observability, Budgets, and Operational Control

**Status:** 0.5.0-draft.2 — proposed, not adopted  
**Audience:** Agent developers, service owners, and operators  
**Applicability:** Every production agent; depth and signals scale with risk, exposure, and execution class.  
**Reader question:** How can a builder give an operator enough evidence to understand a run, prevent bounded work from becoming unbounded, and stop further work without mistaking a diagnostic signal for proof of a safe or authorized result?

## Purpose and applicability

An agent can appear healthy while its task outcome is wrong, incomplete, denied for a legitimate reason, or still unknown. A model call may finish, a retrieval may find a historical document, a tool may time out after accepting work, or retries may consume the time and resource allowance without producing a contract-permitted answer. Ordinary request logging cannot, on its own, tell an operator which of those things happened or whether more work is safe.

This chapter describes a minimum **operational view**: a way to relate a request to a run, its physical attempts, its resource consumption, and its declared outcome. It also describes **operational control**: executable bounds and an authorized means of preventing further agent work. It is proposed guidance, not evidence that any organization has adopted the controls or that an illustrative record has been operated.

The chapter applies even to a short-lived, single-process assistant. Such an assistant can have a small structured run record rather than a tracing platform, but it still needs correlation, meaningful outcomes, runtime limits, and a stop path. A durable or multi-worker service adds the need to preserve logical correlation across resumes and to coordinate only those budgets that actually span workers or runs.

This chapter does not select a telemetry backend, require distributed tracing, set service objectives, define alert thresholds, or grant anyone release or incident authority. Detailed shared-capacity and high-volume admission engineering belong in [8.4 Capacity, Admission Control, and Backpressure](../../engineering-reference/08-model-context-and-resource-engineering/04-capacity-admission-control-and-backpressure.md); performance and cost optimization belong in [8.3 Cost, Latency, and Performance Optimization](../../engineering-reference/08-model-context-and-resource-engineering/03-cost-latency-and-performance-optimization.md). Data handling and audit evidence remain owned by [3.3 Data, Privacy Handling, and Minimum Audit Evidence](../03-security-and-trust-boundaries/03-data-privacy-handling-and-minimum-audit-evidence.md). Evaluation methods are owned by Part 4, release authorization and change control by [5.2 Readiness, Change, and Rollout](02-readiness-change-and-rollout.md), operational ownership and incidents by [5.3 Ownership, Incidents, Maintenance, and Retirement](03-ownership-incidents-maintenance-and-retirement.md), and recovery of uncertain external effects by [7.2 Side Effects, Idempotency, and Unknown Outcomes](../../engineering-reference/07-durable-execution-and-human-governed-work/02-side-effects-idempotency-and-unknown-outcomes.md).

## Working model

A **request** is an initiating interaction. A **run** is the bounded unit of agent work undertaken for that request. One request can create more than one run, and one durable run can pause, resume, or move between workers. Give the run a stable identity and retain its relation to the request throughout those changes. An **attempt** is one physical try at an operation, such as a particular retrieval or model request. A retry is therefore another attempt; it is not a free continuation of the first one.

**Telemetry** is the information gathered so an operator can inspect this behavior. An **event** records a meaningful point in time, such as a pre-dispatch reservation or a stop request. A **metric** is a measurement captured at runtime and aggregated to show a statistical pattern. A **trace**, or an equivalent ordered event sequence, relates the operations that form a request path; a **span** is a unit of work or operation within such a trace. OpenTelemetry describes metrics as aggregate statistical information and traces as request-path context, so they answer different questions rather than replace one another. [1] [2]

Correlation is a logical relationship, not a promise that work stayed in one process or one uninterrupted request. A compact run record can carry a request identity, stable run identity, attempt identity, agent and version identity, effective capability or configuration identity, and operation records. Add workflow, approval, tenant, or similar references only when the concept exists and its use is permitted. OpenTelemetry semantic conventions are one optional common vocabulary for naming operations and data; they do not decide which fields an agent must collect or make collection permissible. [3]

Keep several meanings separate in the record. A **terminal outcome** is the contract-level result returned for the run, such as `complete`, `needs_information`, `declined`, or `failed`. An **operational termination or exhaustion reason** says why the runtime stopped trying, such as a deadline, a retrieval ceiling, an operator stop, or an unresolved in-flight operation. An **operation result** says what happened to one model or tool attempt. **Telemetry success** says whether the observation itself was captured. A contract-defined successful user task, an evaluation conclusion, a release authorization, and any authoritative external effect are further, separate facts. None follows merely because a span ended without error or a dashboard is green.

A useful operational record also distinguishes two kinds of budgets. A **runtime-resource budget** bounds the work a particular run, or a deliberately shared pool of runs, may consume: time, model requests, tool calls, iterations, output, tokens, bytes, or estimated spend. It limits attempted work. An **SLI** is a carefully defined quantitative measure of a service property; an **SLO** is its target; an **error budget** is the permitted amount of SLO noncompliance within a stated observation window. SLI/SLO/error-budget practice is about the experienced reliability of a service and the decisions made from it. It does not substitute for a per-run resource ceiling, and a resource ceiling does not establish an SLO. The choice of SLI, target, window, owner, and decision policy remains an organization-specific decision. [4]

## Decisions and best practices

### Choose signals from operator questions

Start with a decision, not a collection habit. An operator may need to tell whether a run reached its contract-defined outcome, whether a permitted denial is increasing, whether one operation is consuming the elapsed allowance, whether a configured ceiling is being reached, or whether a stop is suppressing new work. For service reliability, the service owner may also choose an SLI that describes a user-relevant good event and an SLO that sets a target. A reliability error budget can then show material SLO loss over its chosen window. It should not be called a model, token, or cost budget.

For each question, identify a small signal, the person or group who can act on it, the decision it informs, and the route when the signal crosses its locally chosen condition. An alert without a named action is noise. SRE guidance similarly frames SLO alerting as a rule that combines an indicator with error-budget information to surface significant events, and evaluates alerting by precision, recall, detection time, and reset time. Those factors are useful design questions, not adopted page thresholds, severity levels, staffing rules, or a release decision. [5]

A minimum view normally includes counts of runs by normalized terminal outcome; elapsed and, when meaningful, active-processing duration; model and tool attempt counts and outcome categories; retry or iteration counts; applicable resource consumption; limit-exhaustion and stop observations; and the agent/version and effective capability/configuration identities needed to interpret the run. Separate active processing from human or durable waiting time. A long approval wait, when approvals exist, should not be reported as model latency; an ephemeral read-only assistant should not invent an approval field merely to satisfy a schema.

Use a compact outcome vocabulary that matches the agent contract. `complete` can mean the contract-defined successful outcome. `needs_information`, `declined`, `failed`, `timed_out`, and `cancelled` describe different conditions and should not be collapsed into a generic error. A `partial` outcome is meaningful only when the contract defines what makes it safe and useful. A specific exhaustion observation can accompany a declared non-success terminal outcome; do not assume that every contract has a terminal `budget_exhausted` state. Connect task success to the success criteria in [1.2 Requirements, Success Criteria, and Measurable Targets](../01-problem-definition-and-planning/02-requirements-success-criteria-and-measurable-targets.md), rather than inferring success from fluent text or a citation.

Metrics need deliberately bounded labels. Their cardinality is the number of distinct attribute combinations, and high-cardinality values such as user identities or raw URL paths can create unbounded metric-aggregation memory. [1] Keep run, request, user, tenant, workflow, free-form prompt, raw URL, and arbitrary error text out of ordinary aggregate dimensions. Use controlled categories such as operation type, declared outcome, or safe error class. Put only necessary correlation detail in protected event or trace records under the data-handling rules in [3.3 Data, Privacy Handling, and Minimum Audit Evidence](../03-security-and-trust-boundaries/03-data-privacy-handling-and-minimum-audit-evidence.md). This is not a reason to move sensitive or unbounded fields into another signal by default, nor is ordinary telemetry the audit record for consequential action.

Diagnostics show what the system recorded. They do not prove that a retrieved source semantically supports an answer, that the source was authoritative for the task, that the requester was entitled to receive it, or that a release was authorized. Those claims require the appropriate boundary evidence and, where needed, the evaluation and decision owners in the linked chapters.

### Make budgets executable

Choose resource dimensions from the task’s possible failure modes. A bounded read-only question may need elapsed time, model-request, retrieval-call, iteration, and response-size ceilings. Another service may also need token, byte, or estimated-spend ceilings. Each applicable budget benefits from a clear unit, scope, measurement point, remaining-allowance behavior, and outcome when it is exhausted. These are proposed design choices, not universal numbers.

Account for each **physical attempt**. Before dispatching a model or tool operation, the runtime can check the applicable remaining allowance. When an estimate is usable before dispatch, it can reserve allowance so concurrent work cannot oversubscribe the same run or shared pool. When the operation is dispatched, record the attempt against the budget even if its later result is a timeout or unknown. Reconcile measured usage after a response or billable report arrives. A reservation is a guard against avoidable new work, not proof of the final charge or result: accepted in-flight work can consume resources after the local limit decision.

Put those controls in the deterministic runtime rather than in a model instruction. A retry consumes the same run budget as the failed attempt. Nested retry paths need the same accounting, or a local retry limit can multiply into unbounded work. If a budget spans conversations, tenants, workers, or otherwise independent runs, it needs shared durable accounting and a defined reservation/reconciliation rule. If it does not span those scopes, a shared ledger adds complexity without improving a per-run control. High-scale coordination and admission design remain the concern of [8.4 Capacity, Admission Control, and Backpressure](../../engineering-reference/08-model-context-and-resource-engineering/04-capacity-admission-control-and-backpressure.md).

Unknown cost remains `unknown`; it is not zero, within budget, or evidence that no charge will arrive. Similarly, unavailable token or byte measurements should be recorded as unknown rather than backfilled with an invented default. When a pre-dispatch price estimate is unavailable, the organization still has to decide whether the relevant cost-controlled operation may be dispatched and under what governance. This chapter does not supply a price list, a provider-specific estimate, a tested ceiling, or a universal answer to that policy question.

### Define a safe exhausted state and a real stop

When a limit is reached, stop **new work within that limit**. Record the measured, estimated, or unknown usage; the dimension that caused the decision; the affected attempt; and the runtime termination reason. Then return the contract-defined result. That may be a declared non-success outcome, a request for more information, a permitted escalation, or a clearly labelled partial result only when the contract makes that partial result safe. Do not trim away material evidence to force a complete answer, retry an authorization denial as though it were a transient fault, or treat an unknown operation result as permission to repeat a potentially unsafe action.

A stop request and a confirmed cancellation are different facts. The stop mechanism can prevent dispatch of subsequent operations, but it may arrive while an operation is already in flight. Mark that operation `cancelled` only when cancellation is confirmed. Otherwise record it as in flight, unresolved, or completed according to the evidence available. An undo, if one is possible, is a separate compensating action and is not implied by stopping. Questions about reconciliation of uncertain external effects belong in [7.2 Side Effects, Idempotency, and Unknown Outcomes](../../engineering-reference/07-durable-execution-and-human-governed-work/02-side-effects-idempotency-and-unknown-outcomes.md).

Document the stop scope and its boundary: one run, new admissions to an agent, or a capability that should no longer be invoked. Identify the authorized operator under the applicable local policy, how a request is recorded, which new work is suppressed, how in-flight work is represented, and what evidence is needed before any later resumption. Re-enabling service is a controlled change owned by [5.2 Readiness, Change, and Rollout](02-readiness-change-and-rollout.md); response and escalation ownership belong in [5.3 Ownership, Incidents, Maintenance, and Retirement](03-ownership-incidents-maintenance-and-retirement.md). A stop is not an incident declaration, recovery authorization, or release authorization.

### Recognize failure modes early

A system can produce technically tidy telemetry and still fail its purpose. For example, a model and retrieval operation can each report a normal operation result while the answer is unsupported. The correction is not to label the run `complete` from operation status alone; retain the distinct task-success and evaluation evidence needed for the claim.

Another frequent error is to treat any two different source values as a current-source conflict. Current material plus a clearly marked superseded historical record is a source-precedence situation, not the equal-authority conflict fixture. The correction is to record source status and the case condition, then apply the declared source-selection rule rather than manufacturing uncertainty or selecting a stale value.

Other failures are operational. Unbounded identifiers in metric labels can make the very aggregate used for alerting unreliable or expensive. Retry handlers that reset an attempt counter can bypass an otherwise sensible ceiling. Per-worker counters can overspend a supposedly shared pool. An operator interface can say “stopped” while an in-flight call remains unknown. A partial answer can look successful after a dashboard collapses terminal outcomes. Each failure is corrected by a record that keeps identity, outcome, reason, attempt, budget scope, and in-flight state distinct, and by exercising that record against the configured control before production use.

### Build an inspectable operating control record

Use the optional [operating control record template](../../templates/5.1/operating-control-record.yaml) to make the design reviewable without imposing a platform or a second normative standard. A builder can develop the record in the following sequence.

1. Define the contract-level terminal outcomes and the exact condition for a successful user task. List the separate runtime termination reasons and operation-result categories that could accompany them.
2. Map the request, stable run, and physical-attempt identities. Add agent/version and effective capability/configuration identity. Add only the workflow, approval, tenant, or other references that exist and are permitted.
3. Name the operator questions, minimal signals, safe aggregate categories, decision owner, and response route. If the service will use an SLI/SLO/error budget, record its specification, measurement limitation, window, and the locally governed decision policy separately from runtime-resource ceilings.
4. For each applicable resource dimension, state the unit, scope, pre-dispatch check or reservation behavior where relevant, in-flight treatment, reconciliation source, and exhausted-state behavior. State when cost or usage remains unknown.
5. Define the authorized stop scope and the representation of requested, confirmed, unresolved, and resumed states. Link rather than duplicate the release, incident, privacy, evaluation, and uncertain-effect owners.
6. Inspect a planned example and the evidence checklist below. A filled illustrative template demonstrates a proposed record shape, not an enabled deployment, an evaluation result, a release decision, an incident, a recovery, or a retirement.

## Requirements

- **OPS-01** For every production run, the system MUST assign a stable run identifier and preserve its relation to the initiating request across recorded operations; it MUST identify the agent version and effective capability/configuration identity needed to interpret the run. Workflow and approval identifiers MUST be recorded only when those concepts apply and their use is permitted.
- **OPS-02** For every production agent, operators MUST be able to distinguish the contract-defined successful outcome from clarification, decline, escalation, cancellation, timeout, budget exhaustion, and failure; the system MUST attribute model and tool operations to the relevant run and record their outcome and duration.
- **OPS-03** Before production use, each agent MUST define and enforce runtime ceilings for applicable per-run dimensions, including elapsed time, model requests or iterations, tool calls, and model/tool output; where token or cost limits apply, those limits MUST be enforced or estimated at the earliest feasible boundary, with unknown cost kept unknown.
- **OPS-04** When a budget spans runs or workers, the responsible service MUST enforce it using shared durable accounting; when any limit is exhausted, the runtime MUST stop new work within that limit, emit the specific exhaustion outcome, and return only a contract-permitted partial result or a declared non-success outcome.
- **OPS-05** For every production agent, an authorized operator MUST have a documented mechanism to stop a run or prevent further agent work; the mechanism MUST define treatment of in-flight operations and preserve unresolved outcomes for reconciliation instead of implying that stopping reverses them.

These obligations complement, rather than replace, the universal risk, access, privacy, evaluation, release, and lifecycle rules. They do not require a tracing product, dashboard product, particular data store, or prompt capture. For a small deployment, a structured run record and a reviewed operating view can be proportionate when they answer the relevant questions and enforce the same applicable controls.

## Worked example

The following is a fictional **illustrative, unexecuted** control design for the shared internal policy assistant. Its companion record uses `example_id: POLICY-BUILD-001`, `use_case_id: internal-policy-assistance`, `packet_id: POLICY-OPS-001`, and `status: illustrative-unexecuted`. It represents neither a production deployment nor an executed evaluation, incident, authorization, recovery, retirement, cancellation, or external effect.

The assistant is read-only. It answers from requester-permitted, approved policy material and returns uncertainty rather than inventing a definitive answer. In the ordinary `travel-policy-current@fixture-2026-10-18.1` world, `employee-uk-104` has `approved-policy-read` permission and may access the current `travel-policy-2026@2026-10-01` source, which says GBP 220, and the approved historical `travel-policy-2024-superseded@2024-01-01` record, which says GBP 200. The current source supports the London policy answer; the superseded source is not authority for that answer. Its presence is therefore a **stale-distraction** condition, not a conflict.

The separate `POLICY-CONFLICT-004` fixture variant is the conflict condition. It adds `travel-policy-2026-conflicting-amendment@2026-10-12`, GBP 260, as an equally authoritative current approved source and grants the same permitted employee access to it. In that hypothetical case, the expected terminal outcome is `needs_information`, because the two current permitted sources conflict. For `contractor-uk-204`, the protected retrieval boundary denies travel-policy access; the expected outcome is `declined`, and no restricted amount may appear in the output. These are fixture expectations, not observed runs or an evaluation conclusion. In particular, they say nothing about a `go`, `conditional_go`, or `no_go` release authorization.

For teaching only, the proposed per-run envelope contains at most three model requests, four retrieval calls, 15 seconds elapsed time, and an optional 12 seconds of active processing. These are not measured performance, price, token, byte, or deployment defaults. The planned record correlates a fictional incoming request to a stable run identity, the candidate identity, the current-selection capability/configuration identity, and each retrieval/model attempt. Aggregate telemetry uses controlled fields such as operation type, terminal outcome, and termination reason; it does not use the requester’s identity or question as a metric label.

Consider the controlled exhaustion case `POLICY-EXHAUSTION-008`. The fixture states that the remaining read-only retrieval budget is exhausted and then returns a controlled retrieval timeout. A planned implementation would stop new retrieval work after the declared stop decision, preserve the last physical attempt and its timeout result, and record a termination reason such as `retrieval_budget_exhausted`. Its contract terminal outcome is `failed`, which remains distinct from the exhaustion reason. It does not invent an amount, cite unavailable evidence, or call a different retrieval to make the record look complete. No real attempt, amount of spend, evaluation judgment, or release decision is asserted by this illustration.

Finally, imagine a stop request while a retrieval is in flight. The planned control prevents a later model or retrieval dispatch. It records the stop request and marks the retrieval cancelled only if that is confirmed. If the record cannot establish whether the retrieval completed, it remains unresolved for review rather than being converted into a clean cancellation. Because the shared assistant is read-only, this illustration has no external write to undo; that narrow fact does not generalize to a consequential write-capable agent.

## Evidence and checklist

Before enabling production traffic, inspect a representative record for a complete request-to-run relation; stable run, attempt, agent/version, and effective capability/configuration identities; operation type, duration, and result; terminal outcome; separately recorded termination reason; and each applicable resource measurement or unknown value. Confirm that a resumed durable run retains its logical run identity while identifying the relevant attempt. Confirm that aggregate labels are bounded and that any necessary detailed diagnostic evidence follows the data-handling boundary in [3.3 Data, Privacy Handling, and Minimum Audit Evidence](../03-security-and-trust-boundaries/03-data-privacy-handling-and-minimum-audit-evidence.md).

Exercise the planned limits, including nested retries and concurrent attempts where the budget is shared. Inspect whether the pre-dispatch check or reservation prevents impermissible new work, whether every physical attempt is attributed, and whether later reconciliation preserves unknown cost or uncertain completion rather than converting either to zero or success. For every exhaustion path, inspect the specific reason, the declared terminal outcome, and the absence of prohibited new work. This evidence establishes only the behavior of the exercised control; it does not establish service reliability, semantic grounding, authorization, or a release decision.

Exercise the stop path during idle and in-flight work. Inspect the authorized scope, suppression of later work, request record, and the distinction between requested stop, confirmed cancellation, unresolved operation, and later resumption. Review the local ownership, incident, release, and recovery links with their named owner chapters rather than treating this operations record as a substitute for those decisions. A parser-valid template or a completed checklist is inspectable design evidence, not proof that the control is effective in production.
